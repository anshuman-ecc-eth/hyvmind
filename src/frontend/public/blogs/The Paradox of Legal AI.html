Word Objects

As lawyers, when we hear about the inner workings of large language models (LLMs), we either switch off or turn away.

That’s unfortunate because the key idea behind them is rather intuitive.

Terms like ‘neural embeddings’, ‘word vectors’ and ‘high dimensional spaces’ can sound like sci-fi.

But beneath them lies a simple formula: King - Man + Woman = Queen.

When we ‘subtract’ man from king, something remains.

It corresponds roughly to the idea ‘monarch’.

To this monarch-like remainder, we ‘add’ woman to reach queen.

That’s more or less it.

By turning words into numbers, LLMs are able to plot them in a geometric space.

This allows them to perform mathematical operations (addition, subtraction, products, graphs, distributions etc.) that can ‘generate’ new combinations.

At scale, this results in bots that seem to ‘chat’ like they have a mind of their own.

But that’s a complex illusion.

They’re simply turning words into arrays, contexts into dimensions and meanings into probability distributions.

It’s a powerful process, but not without limits.

We know that: (1) words can never be perfectly represented as numbers, and (2) meaning is constantly shifting.

Semantic Space

A model’s universe of meanings is called its ‘semantic space’.

In this space, there are unpredictable roads to the same destination.

For instance, we can get to ‘queen’ in any of the following ways:

  • (Authority to Make Law - Modern Law) + Woman
  • Name_set (Freddie Mercury + Brian May + Roger Taylor + John Deacon)
  • Find_highest_common_factor (Catherine + Victoria + Elizabeth + Cleopatra).

If a model is well-trained, it will be able to slot 1 and 3 near ‘monarch’, and 2 near ‘rock bands’.

Depending on the query, it will then retrieve one or the other.

This remarkable ability — to find bests routes through a maze of semantic possibilities — arises because terms are not treated as fixed entities.

If you look inside an LLM, you won’t find a dictionary-like arrangement of words and meanings.

Instead, you’ll see strangely numbered boxes whose ‘meanings’ are derived from their interrelations.

In an important sense, that’s all a language model is — a maze of correlations.

These relations are constantly translated into distances and directions.

This is why LLMs require many ‘dimensions’. It’s a way for them to plot new meanings as they ingest more data.

Note that humans don’t think like this.

We don’t convert words into numbers in order to understand them.

Let’s simplify before moving on.

A language model’s word-objects (also called ‘embeddings’):

  • are numbers in arrays,
  • do not exist in isolation,
  • may or may not correspond to real words or concepts (mostly not),
  • can shift and change with time, and
  • can be combined in various ways.

New embeddings alter the probability-space by introducing fresh relations and short-circuits.

That’s why an LLM’s response keeps changing, even to the same question.

That’s not all.

In order to keep pace with jokes and poems, LLMs need steady streams of fresh data.

This has two implications: (1) If input streams get muddy, the model’s semantic space suffers. (2) If these streams dry up altogether, the model becomes outdated.

Legal Semantic Space

Now, it’s clear from the above that we cannot create ‘legal AI’ without creating a ‘legal semantic space’.

But the question is: how should it relate to a model’s existing semantic space?

Before dreaming about ‘legal AI’ we must clarify the relationship between natural language and legal language.

And here’s the catch, the former cannot become the latter without passing through a filter.

The problem is twofold: (1) this filter is constantly evolving, and (2) it uses the same materials (words and phrases) as natural language.

If you’re wondering whether such a filter even exists, consider two examples around legal personhood:

  • Non-lawyers often ask: how can a company and an infant both be ‘persons’ in law?
  • Lawyers often ask: can a chatbot be treated as a ‘person’ for legal reasons?

One is a necessary rabbit hole. The other is a post-human swamp of moral confusion.

But let that not distract us.

It is important to underscore that both uses of the word ‘person’ are unnatural yet legally coherent.

Stop and think.

How many legal concepts can you list that differ from their ‘natural’ meaning?

One, two, ten..

Infinite?

What of all the definition sections and interpretation clauses?

What of their dependencies and interlinkages?

What of their scope and amendments?

What of the layers upon layers of judicial precedent?

Legal language is it’s own distinct world.

It is interwoven with natural language, but not reducible to it.

How could legal principles and doctrines travel seamlessly between jurisdictions if they did not treat natural language as a mere vehicle?

In mathematical terms, we might say that they exist on different ‘planes’ that — despite having common elements — never actually intersect.

That’s why there is no direct route from natural language processing (NLP) to legal reasoning.

Legal semantics, in short, cannot be mixed with natural semantics.

Sociological Filter

Let’s go a bit deeper.

There’s another element in the filter: the institutional layer of lawyers, judges, parliamentarians and others who have the sociological power of legal-meaning-making.

This does not mean that computational linguistics cannot be harnessed for legal work.

Far from it.

LLMs have already replaced search engines and become the main source of first drafts.

Since legal work is entirely textual, there’s no doubt that AI will continue to transform its three main aspects: reading, writing and research.

Lawyers of the future will be able to:

  • search the entire universe of legal knowledge from the same dashboard,
  • discover and isolate the most relevant portions, and
  • write drafts with accurate references.

We can call these the ctrl+f, zoom-in and auto-complete aspects of legal AI.

They aren’t so much concrete functions as developmental trajectories.

No existing LASPs (legal AI service providers) are working in this direction.

They’re not thinking about a distinct space for legal semantics.

They’re taking the easy road: dump legal texts into existing models > create embeddings > hope for the best.

They’re not asking the central question: can legal concepts be converted into numbers?

They’re also forgetting that legal work is fundamentally adversarial.

It’s impossible to ‘generate’ legal knowledge without passing through contestation and debate.

Moreover, such contestation needs to be open and institutionally sanctioned.

There’s a reason why the three primary sites of legal-meaning-making are the parliament, the courtroom and the classroom.

To be sure, nothing prevents a lawyer from using AI for work. But nothing prevents his adversary from using the same tools to challenge him.

Which brings us to the central paradox:

If we give legal AI the freedom to generate interpretations ‘autonomously’, i.e. without human intervention, then its outputs will be easily challenged through adversarial human intervention, which will in turn prevent them from becoming legal.

We cannot escape this paradox by trying to capture the ‘essence’ of legal reasoning (whatever that might mean).

That’s because language becomes ‘legal’ through sociological, not semantic, properties.

Different judges, parliamentarians, lawyers and academics may support the same legal rule using completely different rationales.

Which tells us that a rule’s ‘validity’ is derived not from the moral or logical reasoning behind it, but from the sociological power vested in specific communities to create and validate legal statements.

In short, LLMs can help lawyers come up with novel reasonings, but these cannot become ‘legal’ on their own.

They must pass through a filtering layer, which is nothing but the community of legal interpreters.

Many LASPs get rid of this human layer because it’s costly, error-prone, and doesn’t scale well.

But there’s no other way to give natural language ‘legal’ attributes.

Removing domain-experts is like killing the goose.

The problem is the exact opposite: how do we get more of them to engage with AI, not as an individual weapon, but as a collective asset.

The labour of legal semantics is ‘costly’ for a reason. It is the bedrock of the entire industry’s value proposition.

The only way to solve the above paradox is to make ‘AI-aided interpretations’ usable out of the box, i.e. without additional verification.

And that requires a common semantic layer which everyone can trust.
